\section{The increment of capital supply}\label{sec:inc}

Section~\ref{sec:mfg} ends by closing a route and leaving the original target: uniqueness through
single crossing, which the programme has pursued through Light's Theorem 1 and cannot get above
$\mu\approx3.4$. This section changes what has to be proved. Theorem 1 need not hold. It need only
fail on little mass.

\subsection{What single crossing actually asks}

Work in wage units, where homotheticity makes capital supply per unit of wage a function of the rate
alone and clearing reads $S(r)=D(r)$ with $D(r)=\alpha/((1-\alpha)(r+\delta))$. Excess supply is
increasing --- which is all single crossing needs --- as soon as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inc}
  S(r_2)-S(r_1)\;>\;D(r_2)-D(r_1),\qquad r_1<r_2,
  \tag{I}
\end{equation}
that is, as soon as supply does not fall \emph{faster} than demand. Demand is steep at the
calibration, and that is the whole opportunity: over the proved bracket $r\in[2\%,8\%]$ supply's
worst secant slope is $+33$ at $\gamma=5$ against $-54$ for demand, and it never bends backwards at
any $\gamma$ up to $12$. A proof of \eqref{eq:inc} may be lossy by more than the true increment and
still close.

The two-sided bracket on the \emph{level} of capital that the existence theorem supplies cannot be
used for this. As $r_2-r_1\to0$ the bracket-only condition $F(r_2)-C(r_1)>D(r_2)-D(r_1)$ degenerates
into $F\ge C$, a bracket of zero width; measured, its margin runs from $-352$ to $-1737$. The object
to bound is the increment, not the level twice.

The two-sided bracket on the \emph{level} of capital that the existence theorem supplies
cannot be used for this, for a reason that recurs below and is set out in
Appendix~\ref{app:routes}: a bracket as wide as the quantity being bounded contributes a
spurious spread larger than the real one.

\subsection{Coupling the two economies}

Run both economies on a common earnings path from the common start $a_0=0$, and let
$x_j=a_j(r_2)-a_j(r_1)$. Split the step at the \emph{lower}-rate state:
\[
  x_{j+1}=\underbrace{\bigl[g^2_j(a^2_j)-g^2_j(a^1_j)\bigr]}_{\text{same policy, different assets}}
   +\underbrace{\bigl[g^2_j(a^1_j)-g^1_j(a^1_j)\bigr]}_{\text{same state, different rate}} .
\]
The second bracket is the failure of Theorem 1 at that state, which
(\lean{stagePolicy\_le\_iff\_consumption\_le}) is exactly $c^2-c^1\le(r_2-r_1)a$ read with a slack;
call its shortfall $e_j$. The first is at least $-L_j\,(x_j)^-$ when saving is nondecreasing in
assets and Lipschitz with constant $L_j$. So
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec}
  x_{j+1}\;\ge\;-L_j\,(x_j)^-\;-\;e_j .
  \tag{$\ast$}
\end{equation}
Nothing in \eqref{eq:rec} asks $x_j\ge0$: the pointwise ordering of policies is not assumed, and its
failure is priced by $e_j$ rather than excluded.

\begin{theorem}[The gap envelope]\label{thm:envelope}
If $x_0\ge0$, $L_j\ge0$, $e_j\ge0$ and \eqref{eq:rec} holds at every age, then $x_j\ge-f_j$ where
$f_0=0$ and $f_{j+1}=L_jf_j+e_j$; hence $\sum_jx_j\ge-\sum_jf_j$. With demand falling by more than
the average of $f$, \eqref{eq:inc} follows.
\formal{OLG.gapAccum}, \lean{OLG.negPart\_le\_gapAccum}, \lean{OLG.neg\_gapAccum\_le},
\lean{OLG.sum\_neg\_gapAccum\_le}, \lean{OLG.lt\_of\_increment\_bound}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The mass-weighted form}

The negative part is the point of the formulation. From \eqref{eq:rec},
\[
  (x_{j+1})^-\;\le\;L_j\,(x_j)^-+e_j ,
\]
whose right-hand side is \emph{linear} in the two quantities. Expectations therefore pass straight
through it, and averaging over the cohort replaces $e_j$ by the \textbf{mass-weighted} failure of
Theorem 1, $\bar e_j=\mathbb E_{1}\bigl[(g^1_j-g^2_j)^+\bigr]$, in place of its worst case over
states. Since the violation is confined to a sliver of the distribution, that is the difference
between a bound that fails and one that passes by two orders of magnitude.

\subsection{The Lipschitz constant}

The engine is proved, and so is the Lipschitz constant it runs on.

\subsubsection*{The propensity floor}

\begin{theorem}[The propensity to consume is at least $\kappa_k$]\label{thm:mpcfloor}
At every stage, every income state and every pair of asset levels in the region,
\[
  \kappa_k\,(1+r)(a_2-a_1)\;\le\;c_k(z,a_2)-c_k(z,a_1),\qquad a_1\le a_2 ,
\]
so saving is Lipschitz in assets with constant $(1-\kappa_k)(1+r)$, which is the $L_j$ the increment
argument uses.
\formal{IncomeFluctuation.stageMPC\_mul\_le\_stageConsumption\_sub}
\end{theorem}

The terminal condition is an identity rather than an assumption: in the last period consumption is
cash on hand, so the propensity is exactly $1=\kappa_0$. The step is the two-point Euler comparison.
With $b_i$ the saving at $a_i$ and $t=\kappa_kR(b_2-b_1)\ge0$, the induction hypothesis raises next
period's consumption by at least $t$ in \emph{every} state, so the Euler equation above the floor at
$a_2$ gives
\[
  c_2^{-\gamma}\;\le\;\beta R\sum_{z'}\pi\,\bigl(C'(z')+t\bigr)^{-\gamma}
   \;=\;\beta R\,M(C'+t)^{-\gamma}\;\le\;\beta R\,\bigl(M(C')+t\bigr)^{-\gamma},
\]
with $M$ the certainty equivalent, the power mean of exponent $-\gamma$. The Euler equation under
the cap at $a_1$ gives $M(C')\ge\Thorn c_1$, and $\beta R=\Thorn^\gamma$ turns the chain into
$c_2\ge c_1+t/\Thorn$, which is the claim at stage $k+1$ once denominators are cleared.

The last inequality in the display is \textbf{superadditivity of the certainty equivalent},
$M(x+t)\ge M(x)+t$, which is concavity and homogeneity of degree one at the midpoint
(\lean{powerMean\_add\_const\_le}, already proved for the constraint-incidence bound of
Section~\ref{sec:thm1}). It is worth pausing on: this is the direction a negative power mean
obliges, and it is the reverse of what several arguments in this programme wanted and could not
have. The recurring obstruction, read the other way round, is what makes the floor go through.

Measured, the floor holds at every stage and every reached state with equality only at the last
stage, and the constant is nearly tight: the true accumulation is $20.7$ against the sandwich's
$21.2$.

\subsubsection*{The Lipschitz bound it gives}

\begin{theorem}[Accumulated assets are Lipschitz in assets]\label{thm:cohortlip}
For $a_1\le a_2$ in the region,
\[
  A_k(a_2,z)-A_k(a_1,z)\;\le\;G_k\,(a_2-a_1),
\]
with $G_k$ the slope \lean{cohortSlope} of the affine ceiling: $G_0=1$,
$G_{k+1}=1+G_k(1-\kappa_{k+1})(1+r)$.
\formal{IncomeFluctuation.cohortAssets\_sub\_le\_cohortSlope}
\end{theorem}

The induction is the one already written for the affine floor, with one substitution: the propensity
floor turns a step of assets into a step of saving no larger than $(1-\kappa_{k+1})(1+r)$ times it,
and the expectation over next period's earnings passes the constant through unchanged. The same $G_k$
that bounds the \emph{level} of accumulated assets in Section~\ref{sec:eqm} therefore bounds their
\emph{differences}, which is what the increment argument accumulates --- and it is the $L_j$ whose
accumulation was measured at $21$ above.

\subsection{The mass-weighted failure}

What is left is $\bar e_j$, an expectation against a distribution nobody controls. It looks like the
hard part, and it would be if the failure of Theorem 1 were spread over assets. It is not.

Measured, the failure of Theorem 1 is not spread over assets but confined to the
high-earnings states --- at $\gamma=5$ the top two of seven, carrying invariant mass $0.095$.
That is the coordinate in which no estimate is needed.

\subsubsection*{Why that coordinate is free}

Earnings are exogenous and the cohort is born with its state drawn from the invariant law, so the
earnings marginal is $\nu$ at \emph{every} age, exactly. Nothing has to be estimated in that
coordinate.

\begin{theorem}[The earnings marginal of a cohort is $\nu$]\label{thm:marginal}
If $\nu$ is invariant for $\pi$, the $\nu$-weighted expectation of a function of the earnings state
is unchanged by any number of steps of the cohort recursion. Hence
$\bar e_j\le\sum_z\nu_z\,V_j(z)$ with $V_j(z)$ the supremum over assets of the failure at earnings
state $z$: exact in earnings, worst-case only in assets.
\formal{IncomeFluctuation.incomeStep}, \lean{IncomeFluctuation.stageStep\_of\_incomeFn},
\lean{IncomeFluctuation.sum\_mul\_incomeStep}, \lean{IncomeFluctuation.sum\_mul\_incomeStep\_iterate},
\lean{IncomeFluctuation.sum\_mul\_le\_of\_le}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The two-rate difference}

One quantity remains: $V_j(z)$, the largest failure of Theorem 1 over assets at a single earnings
state. There is no distribution in it, and the accumulation leaves an allowance of
$0.670/21\approx0.032$ per age for $\sum_z\nu_zV_j(z)$. Two routes to it suggest themselves and both
fail --- a quantitative version of the Euler step, which amplifies, and a direct bound from the
sandwich, which is an order of magnitude too loose (Appendix~\ref{app:routes}). What works is a
different chaining of the same two Euler relations.

\subsubsection*{Chaining through the certainty equivalent}

That amplification is not intrinsic to comparing the two rates; it is an artefact of how the
expectation was bounded. The minimum-based step \eqref{eq:qstep} of Appendix~\ref{app:routes}
replaces $\sum\pi\,C^{-\gamma}$ by its smallest term,
which charges every stage's error against next period's consumption in the \emph{worst} earnings
state. Chaining the same two Euler relations through the certainty equivalent instead leaves no
minimum anywhere:

\begin{proposition}[Certainty-equivalent chaining]\label{prop:cechain}
From $c_2^{-\gamma}\ge K_2M_2^{-\gamma}$ and $c_1^{-\gamma}\le K_1M_1^{-\gamma}$,
\[
  \frac{c_2}{c_1}\;\le\;\Bigl(\frac{K_1}{K_2}\Bigr)^{1/\gamma}\frac{M_2}{M_1},
\]
with $M_i$ the certainty equivalent of next period's consumption.
\formal{CertaintyEquivalent.mul\_le\_mul\_of\_euler\_pair}
\end{proposition}

Propagating a constant shift through $M$ then costs the power mean's gradient sum
$A=\sum_{z'}\pi\,(C/M)^{-\gamma-1}$, which is $1$ exactly when next period's consumption is riskless.
So the per-stage multiplier becomes a measure of the dispersion of next period's consumption under the
risk-tilted measure, rather than the ratio of today's consumption to its worst realisation:

\subsubsection*{The propensity floor damps the step}

The factor of $1.02$ closes once the propensity floor is put into the step, where it belongs. Under
the failure hypothesis the household at the lower rate saves MORE, by exactly the failure $D$, and
saving more raises next period's consumption by at least $\kappa_kR_1D$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:mpcfloor}). Carrying that through,
\[
  D\Bigl[1+\frac{qA\kappa_kR_1}{\Thorn_1}\Bigr]\;\le\;\frac{qA}{\Thorn_1}\varepsilon_k
   +(q-1)c_1+\frac{qA}{\Thorn_1}\Delta r\,b_2-\Delta r\,a ,
\]
so the error carried from one stage to the next is multiplied not by $qA/\Thorn_1$ but by
\[
  m_k\;=\;\frac{qA/\Thorn_1}{1+qA\kappa_kR_1/\Thorn_1}\;=\;\frac{qA}{\Thorn_1+qA\kappa_kR_1},
\]
at most one exactly when $qA\le\Thorn_1+qA\kappa_kR_1$
(\lean{CertaintyEquivalent.damped\_le\_one}). The damping is the propensity floor feeding back
against the failure.

\subsubsection*{The multiplier is a variance}

The multiplier $A$ has to be bounded from primitives, and what does that is the exact constraint
$M$ carries rather than any bound on the spread of $C$ (Appendix~\ref{app:routes} collects the
attempts that use the spread). Writing $u=(C/M)^{-\gamma}$ for the risk
tilt, the definition of the power mean says $\mathbb E_w[u]=1$ \emph{exactly}, and the gradient sum
is $A=\mathbb E_w[u^{1+1/\gamma}]$. At $\gamma=1$ the exponent is two and

\begin{proposition}[The gradient sum is one plus the variance]\label{prop:gradvar}
With $\mathbb E_w[u]=1$, $\;\mathbb E_w[u^2]=1+\operatorname{Var}_w(u)$.
\formal{sum\_mul\_sq\_eq\_one\_add\_variance}
\end{proposition}

so $A=1+\operatorname{Var}_w(u)$, with no inequality anywhere. Above $\gamma=1$ the exponent falls
below two and Lyapunov gives $A\le(1+\operatorname{Var}_w(u))^{(\gamma+1)/(2\gamma)}$.

That Lyapunov step interpolates between the zeroth and second moments and ignores that the first
moment is exactly one. Interpolating between the first and second instead gives the sharp bound.

\begin{proposition}[The sharp moment bound]\label{prop:sharpmom}
For weights summing to one, $u\ge0$ with $\mathbb E_w[u]=1$ and $1<p<2$,
$\;\mathbb E_w[u^p]\le(\mathbb E_w[u^2])^{p-1}$, with equality for the two-point law on
$\{0,\,\mathbb E_w[u^2]\}$. With $p=1+1/\gamma$: $A\le(1+\operatorname{Var}_w(u))^{1/\gamma}$.
\formal{sum\_mul\_rpow\_le\_rpow\_sum\_mul\_sq}
\end{proposition}

It is Jensen for the concave $x\mapsto x^{p-1}$ under the tilted weights $w_iu_i$, which sum to one
by hypothesis; or, in the language of the classical moment problem, the lower principal representation
of the Chebyshev system $\{1,x,x^2\}$ for $x^p$ with $p\in(1,2)$. The exponent $1/\gamma$ is below
Lyapunov's $(\gamma+1)/(2\gamma)$ for every $\gamma>1$ and equal to it at $\gamma=1$. A lower bound
on $u$ sharpens it further --- the extremal law becomes $\{u_{\min},\,1+\operatorname{Var}/(1-u_{\min})\}$
--- but by less. Against the allowance (\texttt{numerics/sharpmom.m}):

That is a complete route at logarithmic utility, and a sharp statement of what it needs. It would give

\subsubsection*{The two-regime bracket}

So the variance of the risk tilt is what must be bounded, and bounding it needs an admissible set
for $C$ tight enough that its worst member satisfies the requirement. Read as a bound, the constrained
regime's identity supplies a \emph{ceiling}: consumption never exceeds cash on hand, so at every
state
\[
  \underbrace{\kappa_k\bigl(m+H^{\mathrm{risk}}_k(z')\bigr)}_{L(z')}\;\le\;C(z')\;\le\;
  \underbrace{\min\bigl\{\kappa_k(m+H_k(z')),\;m\bigr\}}_{U(z')},\qquad m=y(z')+Rb,
\]
both sides primitive, together with the slope constraints $\kappa_k\Delta y\le\Delta C\le\Delta y$ that
link the states. Maximising $\operatorname{Var}_w(M/C)$ over that admissible set changes the picture
completely. The best feasible profile found gives $0.0406$ at the newborn stage --- essentially the
true variance of $0.0404$ --- against an allowance of $0.0661$, and a search over the set turns up
nothing above it. The slope constraints are what do the work: dropping them and maximising over the
box alone gives $0.1537$.

It is not a certificate, and the gap is worth being exact about. What is established rigorously is an
\emph{interval}: the true consumption profile is feasible, so the maximum is at least $0.0411$; the
polytope sits inside the box, so the maximum is at most $0.1537$. The allowance of $0.0661$ lies
inside that interval. The search says the maximum is at the lower end and the chain closes; the
search is not exhaustive, and at two of the three stages tested it returns a value below the true
variance, which shows directly that it can miss.

Closing this needs an upper bound on the maximum that uses the weights. The weight-free one is
available and useless: since $\mathbb E_w[u]=1$, $\operatorname{Var}_w(u)\le\max u-1\le
\mathrm{HM}(U)/\min L-1$, which is $0.97$ at the binding stage against an allowance of $0.066$.

\subsubsection*{The coefficient of variation}

The certificate wants an upper bound on the maximum, and the coordinates matter for getting one. Put $v_i=1/C_i$. Put $v_i=1/C_i$. Then $M=1/\sum_jw_jv_j$ and $u_i=Mv_i$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cv}
  \operatorname{Var}_w(u)\;=\;\frac{\sum_iw_iv_i^{2}}{\bigl(\sum_iw_iv_i\bigr)^{2}}-1 ,
  \tag{CV}
\end{equation}
the squared coefficient of variation of $v$ under $w$ --- exact algebra, with the mean eliminated
rather than bounded. The box in $C$ is a box in $v$, the slope constraints are still linear in $C$,
and \eqref{eq:cv} is a ratio of quadratic forms, scale-invariant, of the kind whose maximum over a
polytope is a standard finite computation.

\subsubsection*{Why enumeration is legitimate}

In $v$-coordinates the objective is coordinatewise quasi-convex, and that is what makes enumeration
legitimate. Holding all but one coordinate fixed,
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial v_j}\frac{\sum_iw_iv_i^{2}}{(\sum_iw_iv_i)^{2}}
  \;\propto\;v_jB-A,\qquad A=\sum_{i\ne j}w_iv_i^{2},\quad B=\sum_{i\ne j}w_iv_i,
\]
so the objective is unimodal with a \emph{minimum} in each coordinate: its maximum over any interval
is at an endpoint. At a maximiser every coordinate therefore sits at an endpoint of its conditional
feasible range.

\subsubsection*{Concavity is the missing constraint}

The bracket and the slope constraints are not by themselves enough: enumerating the admissible set
turns up a profile with variance $0.0874$, above the allowance of $0.0661$
(Appendix~\ref{app:routes}). The profiles that do the damage zig-zag across earnings states, and they
are not consumption functions: consumption is \emph{concave} in cash on hand, by Carroll--Kimball, which this development
already proves (\lean{concaveOn\_stageConsumption}). Across earnings states at fixed assets, cash on
hand moves with $y$, so concavity says the slopes $\Delta C/\Delta y$ fall with $j$ --- five more
linear constraints that the bracket was silently discarding.

With them the same two-directional enumeration gives $0.0415$ at every stage tested, against
allowances of $0.0661$, $0.0725$ and $0.0969$: a margin of $1.59$ to $2.34$ times.

\subsubsection*{The certificate in exact arithmetic}

A first run of this certificate in floating point, over a grid of asset values, leaves two gaps:
the arithmetic, and the sampling (Appendix~\ref{app:routes}). Both close. The calibration is rebuilt
so that every constant is rational: the
earnings chain is Rouwenhorst rather than Tauchen, whose transition probabilities are rational
functions of $\rho=9/10$ and whose invariant law is binomial, and the earnings levels are rational,
normalised to mean exactly one. At $\gamma=1$ everything downstream stays rational ---
$\Thorn=\beta R_1=624/625$, $q=R_1/R_2=52/53$, the propensities $\kappa_k$ by their recursion,
arithmetic human wealth by averages and risk-adjusted human wealth by harmonic means --- so the
allowance is an exact rational number,
\[
  \frac{\Thorn}{q\,(1-\kappa_{J-1}R_1)}-1\;=\;0.066143400439\ldots
\]
The computation is carried in integers over $10^{12}$ with every operation rounded \emph{outward}, so
each quantity is a true enclosure; and the asset argument is carried as intervals covering its whole

cannot save more than $y_{\max}$.

The branch and bound certifies every node: seven earnings states, the asset range split into four

It survives a much larger asset range than the newborn needs. Rerun over $b\in[0,12]$ in eight

\subsubsection*{Every stage}

Running the certificate at each stage in turn --- each with its own $\kappa_k$, its own arithmetic and
risk-adjusted human wealth, its own allowance, and the asset argument as intervals over $[0,12]$ in
four pieces --- certifies all fifty-nine of them. The allowance falls monotonically from $1.168$ at

The cost is where the difficulty lives, and it does not plateau. The early stages are nearly free ---

\subsubsection*{What is settled}

At the rational calibration, at logarithmic utility, over $r\in[4\%,6\%]$: the variance is certified
below what the damping allows, in exact arithmetic, at every stage and over the whole asset range.
With the eight formalised lemmas of this section and Section~\ref{sec:thm1}, Theorem 1 holds at every
state and every age; capital supply rises with the rate; the stationary equilibrium is unique.

What that is, precisely, is a theorem with a computational hypothesis discharged by certificate rather
than by proof term --- the same standing as the existence bracket of Section~\ref{sec:eqm}, and a
weaker standing than the lemmas it rests on. The calibration is one calibration: $\beta=24/25$,
$\rho=9/10$, seven earnings states, sixty periods, $r\in[4\%,6\%]$, logarithmic utility. Nothing here
extends to $\gamma>1$, where Section~\ref{sec:thm1} shows the certainty-equivalent side of the
argument fails from $\gamma\approx1.5$ on. The log case is closed; the rest is not.

It has since been superseded at $\mu=1$. Theorem~\ref{thm:lightind} proves Theorem 1 at every state
and age for $\gamma\le1$ at every calibration, by Light's induction on the stage values, with no
computation; the certificate remains as the record of the increment route, which is the only one
that says anything above $\gamma=1$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:sharpmom} says how far its two-rate
estimate can go. The route itself goes further, with the estimate replaced by an enclosure.

\subsubsection*{The enclosure certificate: $\mu=2,3,5$}\label{sec:enclcert}

bound on the failure of order $0.01$, and Appendix~\ref{app:routes} records that no primitive ceiling
on consumption supplies one. A rigorous enclosure of the two policies does
(\texttt{numerics/rational/encl.py}). It is the endogenous-grid recursion carried in integers over
$10^{15}$ with every operation rounded outward. The stage-$k+1$ consumption at $(a,z)$ with
$m=y_z+Ra$ is $m$ where $\Psi_k(0)\ge m$ and otherwise the unique solution of $c=\Psi_k(m-c)$,
$\Psi_k(a')=\bigl[\beta R\sum_{z'}\pi(z,z')\,c_k(a',z')^{-\gamma}\bigr]^{-1/\gamma}$ (the two Euler
inequalities, Theorems~\ref{thm:sandwich}'s ingredients); $\Psi_k$ is increasing in $a'$ and in

far above the reach of any cohort, because looseness beyond the grid's top creeps inward by a factor
$1/R$ per stage. The failure at age $j$ and state $z$ is then bounded over grid intervals in the

What that establishes is a statement about one \emph{pair} of rates. At the rational calibration,
at $\gamma\in\{2,3,5\}$ --- Aiyagari's $\mu=3$ and $\mu=5$ --- the increment \eqref{eq:inc} holds

$4\%$ and $6\%$ are not both stationary equilibria. That is the pairwise form of single crossing.
Uniqueness on the bracket would need the increment for every pair in it, and the certificate as run
does not give that: for closer pairs the budget $D(r_1)-D(r_2)$ falls in proportion to the gap while
the grid-interval slack in the failure bound does not, so a mesh of pairs would need a finer grid
as the mesh is refined. What is certified is a theorem with one computational hypothesis discharged
by certificate, the standing the logarithmic case had before Theorem~\ref{thm:lightind}. The step
itself is a theorem:

\begin{theorem}[The interval Euler step]\label{thm:intervaleuler}
Let $E_k(x)=\beta R\sum_{z'}\pi(z,z')\,x(z')^{-\gamma}$ and $m=y_z+Ra$. If $0<c_L\le m$ and
$E_k(\ell)\le c_L^{-\gamma}$ for numbers $\ell(z')\le c_k(m-c_L,z')$, then $c_L\le c_{k+1}(a,z)$; if
$0<c_H\le m$, $m-c_H$ lies in the region, and $c_H^{-\gamma}\le E_k(h)$ for numbers
$h(z')\ge c_k(m-c_H,z')$, then $c_{k+1}(a,z)\le c_H$; and if $E_k(\ell)\le m^{-\gamma}$ for
$\ell(z')\le c_k(0,z')$ then $c_{k+1}(a,z)=m$. Between grid points a concave nondecreasing function
lies above the chord of lower bounds at two points and below the left secant extended forward and
the right secant extended backward.
\formal{IncomeFluctuation.le\_stageConsumption\_of\_cert,
IncomeFluctuation.stageConsumption\_le\_of\_cert,
IncomeFluctuation.stageConsumption\_eq\_resources\_of\_cert, le\_of\_chord, le\_of\_left\_secant,
le\_of\_right\_secant}
\end{theorem}

The proofs are the two Euler inequalities of Section~\ref{sec:lc} and monotonicity: should today's
consumption fall below $c_L$ the household saves more than $m-c_L$, its Euler equation holds above
the floor, and monotone consumption tomorrow chains into $c^{-\gamma}\le c_L^{-\gamma}$; the upper
half runs the same way through the Euler inequality under the cap. Every inequality the certificate
checks is an instance of one of these, and what the computation adds is the rational arithmetic,
rounded outward, and the choice of which grid points to use. Everything downstream of the failure
bound was already formalised, so the standing of the result is now exactly that of the
existence bracket: a theorem whose one computational hypothesis is arithmetic. The certificate is
\texttt{numerics/rational/cert.py}, with the calibration in \texttt{inputs.py}. Numerics: \texttt{cestep.m}, \texttt{damp.m},
\texttt{nearfar.m}, \texttt{lyap.m}, \texttt{varbnd.m}, \texttt{varmpc.m}, \texttt{varmax.m},
\texttt{slopestate.m}, \texttt{tworeg.m}, \texttt{polymax.m}, \texttt{bandb.m}, \texttt{vertcert.m},
\texttt{vertfull.m}, \texttt{certify.m}.

\subsubsection*{Finiteness, differentiability and the slope of supply}\label{sec:finite}

Uniqueness being out of reach above $\gamma=1$, the weaker question is whether the number of
equilibria is finite. Differentiability of $r\mapsto S(r)$ is the wrong property to ask for, in two
ways. It is false: each stage policy has a kink at the wealth where the borrowing constraint stops
binding, kinks propagate backward to wherever a policy sends a household onto a kink of the next
stage, and $S$ inherits finitely many kinks in $r$, at the rates where a cohort's asset path crosses
one. And it would not help: a smooth excess demand can vanish on a Cantor set. What $S$ has is
more: under CRRA utility and a finite chain the interior branch of every policy is real-analytic in
$(a,r)$ by the analytic implicit function theorem applied to the Euler equality, the constrained
branch is affine, and supply is a finite composition of these along the newborn's paths, so $E=S-D$
is piecewise real-analytic in $r$ with finitely many pieces, each piece the restriction of a
function analytic on a neighbourhood. That is the property with a consequence:

\begin{theorem}[Finitely many equilibria on a bracket]\label{thm:finite}
Let $E$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and agree on each of finitely many closed pieces
$[t_i,t_{i+1}]$, $t_0=a\le\dots\le t_n=b$, with a function analytic on a preconnected open
neighbourhood of the piece. If no piece's analytic extension vanishes identically, $E$ has finitely
many zeros in $[a,b]$.
\formal{PiecewiseAnalyticOn, PiecewiseAnalyticOn.finite\_zeros, finite\_zeros\_of\_analyticOnNhd}
\end{theorem}

The proof is the identity theorem on each piece and compactness: an infinite zero set has an
accumulation point, at which the extension vanishes on a punctured neighbourhood, hence on the
whole connected neighbourhood. The piecewise analyticity of $S$ itself is not formalised; the
theorem records exactly what it would buy. What it buys is a reduction. $E$ vanishes identically on
a piece only if $S'=D'$ there, and $D'(r)=-\alpha/((1-\alpha)(r+\delta)^2)$ is $-39$ in wage units
at $4\%$ and $-22$ at $8\%$. Finiteness at the calibration therefore follows from a bound on how
fast supply can \emph{fall}: any provable $S'(r)>D'(r)$ on the bracket excludes a flat piece. A
bound of that kind is also, and more importantly, a proof of uniqueness: $E$ is continuous and
piecewise $C^1$, so $E'>0$ off the finitely many kinks makes $E$ strictly increasing, and there is
at most one equilibrium. The increment route of Section~\ref{sec:enclcert} is this argument at finite
$\Delta r$; its grid slack does not vanish with $\Delta r$, which is why it is pairwise. The
derivative form has no grid slack.

\[
\frac{\partial c}{\partial r}
=\frac{-c/(\gamma R)+\Psi_x\,a+\sum_{z'}w_{z'}\,\partial_rc_k(m-c,z')}{1+\Psi_x},
\qquad
\frac{\partial g}{\partial r}=a-\frac{\partial c}{\partial r},
\]
and $\partial_rc=a$ at the corner. The failure of Theorem 1 in derivative form is

$S'(r)\ge-\frac1J\sum_kG_k\sum_z\nu_z\max_{a\le\text{reach}}e_{k+1}(a,z)$. Upper and lower bounds

\paragraph{The certified recursion.} That version is built for a single rate
(\texttt{numerics/rational/derivcert.py}): the weighted recursion in exact rational arithmetic on
the enclosures of Section~\ref{sec:enclcert}, with the bounds on $\partial_rc_k$ carried on grid
\emph{intervals}, since a derivative has no monotonicity to interpolate with. On an interval
$[a_i,a_{i+1}]$ the consumption lies between the enclosure at its ends, the saving between
$m(a_i)-\overline c(a_i)$ and $m(a_{i+1})-\underline c(a_{i+1})$ (saving is monotone), tomorrow's
consumption in the enclosure at the ends of that range, each weight between its two extreme values,
$\Psi_x$ in $[0,(\sum w)R]$, and the bound on $\partial_rc_k$ over the range the largest on the grid
intervals it meets. The interior formula needs the Euler equality, so it is used only where the
saving is positive on the whole interval ($m(a_i)-\overline c(a_i)>0$, monotone saving); on every
other interval, and on the certified corner, $\partial_rc=a$ is covered as well.

The first run of it missed. At $\gamma=3$ and $4\%$ the bound was $43$ against the $39$ needed, at
$6\%$ $83$ against $29$, and at $\gamma=5$ $188$ against $39$, the failure landing at ages five to ten
where the true derivative-form failure is exactly zero. The recursion was not at fault --- with exact
ingredients it gives $4$ --- the enclosures were: their widths were $10^{-4}$ up to the twenty-fifth
stage and then grew by a seventh per stage to $0.07$ at the newborn stage, and a perturbation of that
size in the weights of a sixty-stage linear recursion compounds to a factor of three. The growth had
a cause and a fix. The certificate's candidate for each fixed point was computed on the midpoint of
the enclosure, so the one-step certified refinement landed a fraction $\Psi_x$ of the width short of
the certified fixed point at every stage; computing the candidates for the lower and upper fixed
points on their own sides removes the loss, and cuts the newborn-stage width from $0.07$ to $0.02$
--- the width the Lean checker, which bisects, had all along. What remains is interpolation slack of
order $c''h^2$ per stage, accumulated over a fifty-stage transient (raising the grid top a hundredfold
changes nothing), and a grid four times finer cuts it sixteenfold, to $0.002$. With that:

So $E'>0$ is certified at $4\%$ and at $6\%$ for $\gamma=3$, and at $4\%$ for $\gamma=5$, by exact rational arithmetic on the
enclosures, with the interval Euler step, monotone saving, the propensity floor and the envelope as
the theorems behind each line. What it does not yet give is $E'>0$ \emph{between} the two rates,
which is what uniqueness on the bracket needs.

\paragraph{From rates to a bracket.} Between two rates the derivative bound must hold at every rate,
so the enclosures must be valid on a \emph{cell} of rates. The cell enclosure of Section~\ref{sec:mesh}
is too loose to feed the recursion, whose weights carry consumption to the power $\gamma+1$; a cell
enclosure that is fed back from the recursion's own derivative bound diverges for the same reason. What
works is to freeze the transport and verify it: assume $|\partial_rc_k|\le M_k:=\lambda U^0_k+\mu$ on
the cell, with $U^0_k$ the bound at the cell's midpoint, transport the midpoint enclosure by
$M_kh/2$, run the recursion on the transported enclosures, and check $U_k<M_k$ strictly wherever an
assumption was made. The set of rates in the cell at which every transported enclosure holds is
closed, contains the midpoint, and is open by the strict bound and the continuity of consumption in
the rate, so it is the whole cell; the assumption is thereby proved and the bound with it. Two
refinements make it run: assumptions are confined to the wealth zone the recursion actually reads
(closed under the saving map by the propensity floor, about $20$ to $110$ in wealth), and an absolute
floor $\mu=1$ covers the near-corner states the cell pass cannot see are constrained. Outside the zone the
enclosure falls back to the propensity floor and cash on hand, which need no assumption. On cells of
$0.005$ percentage points at $\gamma=3$ the assumption verifies with a margin of a third, and on the
fine grid the certified bound on the cell is nine percent above the midpoint's: $6.9$ against $33.3$
at $5\%$ and $8.1$ against $28.7$ at $6\%$, so $E'>0$ holds on those cells. The width is sharp: cells
of $0.02$ points fail the verification by nine percent at every multiplier $\lambda$, because the
transport width scales with $\lambda$ and so does the bound it produces, and cells of $0.1$ points
fail it forty times over. Each cell costs about an hour and a half on the fine grid (the midpoint enclosure, its derivative pass and
the cell's), so $[4\%,6\%]$ was run as two hundred cells of $0.01$ points on seven cores, split
automatically where they fail (\texttt{derivcert.py}, mode \texttt{fixed}; the run is resumable and
its results are one line per cell in \texttt{runs/bracket\_g3\_4to6.txt}). It took about 40 hours
of wall-clock time. No cell needed splitting: every cell certified at its first width, with the
assumption verified at a ratio of at most $0.75$. The largest certified bound on the fall of supply
over any cell is $9.15$, against a demand slope of at least $28.7$ in magnitude; the worst ratio,
$0.32$, sits at the top of the bracket where demand is flattest, and at the bottom the bound is $6.5$
against $39.0$.

\begin{theorem}[$E'>0$ on the bracket, by certificate]\label{thm:bracket}
At $\gamma=3$ and the calibration of Section~\ref{sec:enclcert}, on every cell of the partition of
$[4\%,6\%]$ into two hundred cells the certified bound $B_d$ on $-S'$ is strictly below $|D'|$.
Hence excess demand is strictly increasing on $[4\%,6\%]$, and the model has at most one
equilibrium interest rate in that bracket.
\end{theorem}

Its standing is one step below Theorem~\ref{thm:encllean}. The interval Euler step, monotone
saving, the propensity floor and the envelope are proof terms; the derivative form of the increment
and the continuation argument that discharges the frozen transport are paper arguments of this
section; and the arithmetic is exact rational arithmetic in Python, not yet replayed in Lean.
Replaying it has the shape of Section~\ref{sec:enclcert}: a checker over naturals, one
\texttt{native\_decide}, and a bridge from the checker's inequalities to the theorems behind them,
with the continuation lemma the one genuinely new piece. Theorem~\ref{thm:finite} is not needed for
the conclusion: strict monotonicity of $E$ on the bracket gives uniqueness there directly, and
finiteness with it.

\paragraph{Outside the bracket, by proof term.} Below and above the bracket the level bounds of
Section~\ref{sec:eqm} take over, and there the computation is cheap enough to replay inside Lean.
On a cell of rates $[r_1,r_2]$ every ingredient of the affine ceiling
(Proposition~\ref{prop:affceil}) is monotone in the rate or in the patience factor
$\Thorn=(\beta R)^{1/3}$: $\kappa_k$ rises with $R$ and falls with $\Thorn$, human wealth falls
with $R$, and the power mean in the risk-adjusted human wealth is monotone in its arguments. A
checker (\texttt{EnclCert/Outer.lean}) brackets $\Thorn$ by rational cube-root bounds, runs the
recursions for $\kappa_k$, $\bar H$, $G_k$ and the intercept $\bar F_k$ with each ingredient at the
end of the cell that makes the bound larger, rounding outward over $D=10^{12}$, and compares the
ceiling with $D(r_2)$. Every stored value is re-verified by an exact integer inequality with
denominators cleared, and those inequalities, not the recursions, are what the bridge
(\texttt{OLG/OuterBridge.lean}) turns into the hypotheses of \lean{olgCapital\_le\_cohortCeil}.
The slackness hypothesis of that theorem is discharged from impatience at every stage:
$\kappa_k\ge1-\Thorn/R$ gives it wherever $\Thorn<R$, and where the rate is negative enough that
$y_{\max}+r\bar a\le0$ it holds outright. The floor side is the clamped floor
(Proposition~\ref{prop:clampfloor}) on a grid of $650$ asset levels, read at the grid point below
the certified saving floor since $\Lambda_k$ is monotone in assets, with the reach hypothesis of
\lean{le\_olgCapital\_at} checked at the top of the cell; the cap is $10^6$, which the reach at
$20\%$ needs. The meshes are $165$ cells from $-8\%$ to $2.3\%$, down to $0.005$ points at the
top where the ceiling crosses demand near $2.35\%$, and $121$ cells from $8.7\%$ to $20\%$, the
floor crossing demand near $8.4\%$. Both verdicts are \texttt{native\_decide}
(\lean{lowCheck\_eq\_true}, \lean{highCheck\_eq\_true}, about four minutes of compiled arithmetic
each), and the bridge is checked by the kernel.

\begin{theorem}[{No equilibrium outside $[2.3\%,8.7\%]$}]\label{thm:outer}
For the seven-state life cycle at the rational calibration with $\gamma=3$, $\beta=24/25$ and cap
$10^6$, capital supply is strictly below demand at every rate in $(-8\%,2.3\%]$ and strictly above
it at every rate in $[8.7\%,20\%]$.
\formal{EnclCert.olg7\_supply\_lt\_demand\_low, EnclCert.olg7\_demand\_lt\_supply\_high}
\end{theorem}

With Theorem~\ref{thm:bracket} this leaves $[2.3\%,4\%)$ and $(6\%,8.7\%]$ as the only rates
below $20\%$ neither excluded by proof term nor covered by the slope certificate. The slope
certificate extends to them by machine time alone, about $440$ cells; beyond $20\%$ the question
is about the artefactual cap rather than the economy, since a lifetime of feasible saving at
higher rates exceeds any fixed cap and the formal model is the capped one.

\subsubsection*{Uniqueness up to a tolerance: the mesh of rate cells}\label{sec:mesh}

The pairwise certificate of Section~\ref{sec:enclcert} extends in principle to a bracket: partition
it into cells of width $h$, enclose the stage consumption over each \emph{cell} rather than at a
rate, certify the increment between every two cells at least $m$ apart, and any two equilibrium
rates are then within $mh$ of each other. The cell enclosure is the same recursion with the two
rates at the ends of the cell placed where they hurt: cash on hand $m$ at $R_a$ in the lower test
and at $R_b$ in the upper, $\beta R_b$ in $\Psi^{\mathrm{lo}}$ and $\beta R_a$ in
$\Psi^{\mathrm{hi}}$, since $\Psi_R(m_R-c)$ is monotone in $R$ through both; each fixed-rate
instance is again Theorem~\ref{thm:intervaleuler}. Between cells $i<j$ the failure bound uses the
gap $\Delta r_{\min}=R_{a,j}-R_{b,i}$ and the budget $D(R_{b,i}-1)-D(R_{a,j}-1)$
(\texttt{numerics/rational/enclmesh.py}).

It does not work at any useful width, and the reason is instructive. At $\gamma=3$ over
$[4\%,5\%]$ in ten cells of $0.1$ percentage points, every pair fails: the accumulated bound is
$B\in[4.6,6.5]$ against budgets of at most $0.25$, and $B$ falls only from $5.9$ to $4.6$ as the
gap grows from one cell to seven. The cell enclosures are $0.14$ wide at stage $20$ and $0.36$ at
the newborn stage on $a\le10$, against $2.5\times10^{-4}$ and $0.09$ for an enclosure at a single
rate, while the true spread of consumption across a cell is at most $|\partial_rc|\,h\le0.02$
(\texttt{numerics/dcdr.m}: $\partial_rc\in[-1.8,+20]$ on the reach, about half of $a$ at the top).
The cell enclosure is twenty to seventy times the truth because it prices the budget rate and the
Euler rate at opposite ends of the cell: the wealth effect through $m$ and the substitution and
human-wealth effects through $\Psi$ are large and of opposite sign, and only their sum is small.
Since $B$ is dominated by the young ages at low wealth, where the $-\Delta r\,a$ term buys nothing,
$B$ scales like $7{,}000h$ against a budget of $36\,\Delta r$, so a pair certifies only when the gap
exceeds about $190$ cells: a cluster of $0.2$ percentage points would need cells of
$10^{-5}$, two thousand of them over $[4\%,6\%]$, about forty hours. The route is not wrong, only
expensive, and the expense is exactly the derivative information the cell enclosure throws away.
Carrying a Lipschitz bound in the rate instead --- a point enclosure transported by a certified
$|\partial_rc|$ --- would cut the cell widths to the true spread, but that bound is the weighted
recursion of Section~\ref{sec:finite}, which on its own already gives $E'>0$; the mesh is
superseded by it.
